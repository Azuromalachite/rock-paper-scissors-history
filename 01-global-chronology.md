# 1. The broader history

## Several different things get called "the origin of rock–paper–scissors"

1. Hand games in general, including finger-guessing and drinking games.
2. Games with three choices that defeat one another cyclically.
3. The specific rock–paper–scissors symbols.
4. Their arrival in a particular country.

Evidence for the first does not establish the other three. This is the problem
with the familiar "2,000-year-old Chinese game" story.

## Chronology

| period | evidence and its limits |
| --- | --- |
| Earlier Chinese traditions, discussed in a Ming-period text | Xie Zhaozhe's *Wuzazu* (五雜組) mentions 手勢令, a hand-gesture drinking game. The passage lists several hand and finger representations and says its method of play was unknown to the author. It does not describe modern rock–paper–scissors. |
| Edo-period Japan | Various *ken* games developed, including three-way cyclic games. Japanese references connect these with Chinese hand-game traditions; dates and the relationship to modern *janken* are disputed. |
| Nineteenth-century Japan | Rock–paper–scissors becomes securely identifiable. A Japanese library research guide (National Diet Library collaborative reference database) estimates emergence during 1804–1843, with acknowledged uncertainty. |
| 1895 | Stewart Culin, *Korean Games, with Notes on the Corresponding Games of China and Japan*, describes Japanese *ishi-ken*/*janken* with stone, paper and scissors. A firm documentary anchor. |
| Twentieth century | The game spreads internationally. In the United States a 1935 recreation handbook describes it as "Rochambeau"; later accounts and returning servicemen helped popularise it (John B. Buescher's documentary investigation). |

## Caution on the Chinese evidence

Popular histories routinely turn "an old text mentions a hand game" into
"people played rock–paper–scissors in the Han dynasty". The passage located
does not establish that. Its reference to Shi Hongzhao also makes identifying
its "Later Han" with ancient Han China problematic. The standard 206 BCE–220 CE
origin date should not be repeated as a demonstrated fact.

## The symbols were never fixed

Japan's earlier cyclic games used other symbols: *mushi-ken* used frog, slug
and snake; other games used human and animal characters. The shared principle
was that each choice defeats one opponent and loses to another.
