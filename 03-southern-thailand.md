# 3. Why bird–stone–water (and other variants) in the South?

The best-supported explanation is participation in a **shared peninsular game
tradition**, with transmission between southern Thailand and the
Malaysian/Singaporean region. The precise route and direction are unknown.

## The variation is real, and more complicated than "the South plays one version"

Dated comments from 26–29 June 2008 (Dek-D):

| locality named by the commenter | reported version |
| --- | --- |
| Songkhla | bird–stone–water |
| Hat Yai | bird–stone–water, with the chant วัน ทู ส้ม |
| Trang | bird–gun–water |
| Mueang Nakhon Si Thammarat | bird–gun–water |
| Mueang Surat Thani | scissors–hammer–paper |

These are informal local testimonies, not a representative survey, but their
dates and place names make them useful evidence that the variation was
recognised by 2008.

## The Malaysian parallel is independently documented

- Malaysia's Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka preserves a 7 September 2018 enquiry
  about the spelling of *ohsom / osom / wan tu som*, described by the enquirer
  as a children's game like rock–paper–scissors using stone, water and bird.
  DBP replied that it had not yet recorded a spelling. This establishes
  contemporary usage, not etymology.
- A Malaysian artist's statement recalls childhood *OSOM* with *burung, batu,
  air* (bird, stone, water). Malaysian reporting documents this alongside other
  local versions.
- The same cycle is recorded in Singaporean schoolyard folklore.

| choice | defeats | explanation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| bird | water | the bird drinks it |
| water | stone | the stone sinks |
| stone | bird | the stone strikes the bird |

**Inference:** the Hat Yai–Malaysia match in *both* chant and symbols makes
transmission or shared inheritance persuasive. It is a stronger explanation
than southern children independently choosing birds because birds were around.

The evidence does not yet distinguish between:

- Malaysian communities transmitting it northward;
- southern Thai communities transmitting it southward;
- both inheriting it through an earlier regional network;
- repeated exchanges among multilingual communities.

Nor does it identify the carriers as Malay, Chinese, Thai or any other group.

## Guns, bricks, glasses and other objects

Thai descriptions record a southern game called **ฉู่ฉี้**, listing ปืน น้ำ
ก้อนอิฐ แก้วน้ำ (gun, water, brick, drinking glass). The account is hosted by
Thung Song; a university thesis repeats essentially the same description, so
the two may share one source. Neither gives complete rules or says whether all
four symbols belong to one version.

A Thai account attributed to the book *108 ซองคำถาม* describes a Malaysian
version with bird, stone, gun, board and water (gun beats bird, water beats
gun, …). A useful lead, but its abbreviated rules should not be read as a
standardised five-choice game.

The mechanism is simple: children keep the competitive structure and change
the objects and explanations. Bird–gun–water stays a cycle if gun kills bird,
bird drinks water, and water disables or sinks gun. That explains *how* the
variation works, not who invented it.

"Instead" may also imply an unproven sequence. It has not been shown that
southern communities first played scissors–hammer–paper and then replaced it;
they may have learned a different version from the outset.

## The Phuket glass variant (วันตูโส้ม)

A Facebook post (seen as screenshots, not reproduced here) describes:

| symbol | gesture | defeats | explanation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| หิน stone | closed fist | glass | stone breaks the glass |
| แก้วน้ำ drinking glass | fingers gathered into a cup | water | glass scoops up water |
| น้ำ water | open hand | stone | stone sinks in water |

Comments on the post associate:

| locality | version reported |
| --- | --- |
| Phuket | glass–stone–water |
| Krabi | cup/glass (จอก/แก้ว)–stone–water |
| Hat Yai / Songkhla | bird–stone–water |
| Trang / Nakhon Si Thammarat | bird–gun–water |
| Phatthalung | bird–gun–water (one grouped regional comment) |

These are reports of local practice; they do not establish uniform rules
across a province or a generation.

### The dictionary reference

The post cites *พจนานุกรมภาษาถิ่นภูเก็ต พุทธศักราช 2560*. The book exists:
edited by อนันต์ อารีย์พงศ์, published by โรงเรียนสตรีภูเก็ต for the school's
108th anniversary. Japan's National Diet Library catalogue and a Silpakorn
University bibliography both give its publication year as 2559 / 2016, despite
2560 / 2017 in the title. **The วันตูโส้ม entry itself has not been located**,
so it is unverified whether the post reproduces it accurately.

### Separating description from narrative

| claim in the post | assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Phuket has glass–stone–water | supported by the post and another commenter's locality-specific report |
| วันตูโส้ม is related to Malaysian *one two som* | plausible; matches the regional comparison |
| the expression originated during British colonial rule | not established |
| the game split into exactly three historical "branches" | a proposed classification, not a demonstrated history |
| water represents coastal communities' way of life | an interpretation; symbols alone cannot establish the reason |
| โอน้อยออก developed from Lai Lai Li Tam Plong | needs separate linguistic and historical evidence |

Background that makes a multilingual route plausible (but does not prove it):
the Thailand Cultural Encyclopedia describes Hokkien-speaking migrants bringing
Malay and English vocabulary into Phuket, often after living in Malaya.

One useful implication: **the same chant accompanies different symbol sets**
(วันตูส้ม-type names with both bird–stone–water and glass–stone–water). Chant,
gestures, symbols and rules should be traced separately rather than assumed to
travel together.
