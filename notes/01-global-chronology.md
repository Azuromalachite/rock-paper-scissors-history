# 1. The broader history

## Several different things get called "the origin of rock–paper–scissors"

1. Hand games in general, including finger-guessing and drinking games.
2. Games with three choices that defeat one another cyclically.
3. The specific rock–paper–scissors symbols.
4. Their arrival in a particular country.

Evidence for the first does not establish the other three. This is the problem
with the familiar "2,000-year-old Chinese game" story.

## Chronology

| period | evidence and its limits |
| --- | --- |
| A tenth-century episode, told in a Ming text | Xie Zhaozhe's *Wuzazu* (五雜組, Ming, c. 1600), juan 6, describes 手勢令, a hand-gesture drinking game, at a banquet of Five Dynasties Later Han (947–950) officials. The passage lists names for the palm, joints and each finger, likens it to a children's finger-catching game, and says its method of play was unknown to the author. It does not describe modern rock–paper–scissors. |
| Edo-period Japan | Various *ken* games developed, including three-way cyclic games. Japanese references connect these with Chinese hand-game traditions; dates and the relationship to modern *janken* are disputed. |
| Early nineteenth-century Japan (estimate) | Kako Satoshi (加古里子, 2008) estimates that 石拳 (stone-ken) developed from 虫拳 between Bunka and Tenpō (1804–1843), while stating that its date and process are unknown. This reaches the notes through a Yokohama Central Library answer in the NDL Collaborative Reference Database (横浜市中央2750). It is an estimate, not a dated record. |
| 1895 | Stewart Culin, *Korean Games, with Notes on the Corresponding Games of China and Japan*, pp. 44–45, describes Japanese *ishi-ken*, usually called *janken*, with stone (fist), paper (open hand) and scissors, and the rules. A firm documentary anchor: the earliest secure document of the game in these notes. |
| Twentieth century | The game spreads internationally. In the United States, Ella Gardner's *Handbook for Recreation Leaders* (Children's Bureau Publication 231; transmittal letter December 1935, the digitised copy's imprint 1936), p. 50, describes "Rochambeau" as a **team** game: two lines whose captains choose rock, scissors or paper. An unsigned "Ask a Historian" answer at teachinghistory.org names it as the earliest American mention found and credits returning servicemen after World War II with popularising the game. |

## Caution on the Chinese evidence

Popular histories routinely turn "an old text mentions a hand game" into
"people played rock–paper–scissors in the Han dynasty". The passage does not
establish that. Its reference to Shi Hongzhao (d. 950) places the episode in
the Five Dynasties Later Han, not the Han dynasty. The standard
206 BCE–220 CE origin date should not be repeated as a demonstrated fact.

## The symbols were never fixed

Japan's earlier cyclic games used other symbols. *Mushi-ken*, which Culin
also describes, used frog, slug and snake. Other games used human and animal
characters. The shared principle was that each choice defeats one opponent and
loses to another.

The research handoff of 2026-11-06 adds leads, **none checked here**:

- *kitsune-ken* (fox, village head, hunter), with illustrations reported from
  1774, and a *mushi-ken* illustration from 1809;
- a scholarly claim of a 1756 printed reference to *mushi-ken*;
- a proposed link between *mushi-ken* and a Chinese snake–frog–centipede
  cycle;
- Korean 가위바위보 (scissors, rock, wrapping cloth) and 묵찌빠, which uses
  the same gestures in a different contest;
- Okinawan and Amami frog–slug–snake games (*būsa*, Okinoerabu さみ).

A starting point for the scholarship is Sepp Linhart (1995), "Some Thoughts on
the Ken Game in Japan: From the Viewpoint of Comparative Civilization
Studies", *Senri Ethnological Studies* 40: 101–124, doi:10.15021/00003009.
The symbols, hand shapes, chants, the matching-versus-dominance mechanism and
the match rules may each have their own history. Modern janken should not be
read back into every recorded *ken*.
