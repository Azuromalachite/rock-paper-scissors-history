## A hand duel is not only a rule set

In practice a game cannot be separated from the people playing it, the
relationship it settles, the languages spoken, norms of fairness, and the local
procedures for deciding who starts, who loses and who takes the unwanted role.
For comparison it can still be taken apart **analytically** into features that
may have different histories:

| level | what to record |
| --- | --- |
| round | the simultaneous signs, hand shapes, symbols, tie handling, winner |
| match | how many rounds, the stopping rule, whether scores reset |
| social episode | why the duel was invoked, the larger game around it, whose authority it settles, whether the result is obeyed |

Taking a game apart for analysis does not mean its features travel separately.
Whether they do is an empirical question.

## Match-scoring: four different stopping rules

A correction from the owner's own experience: the Thai convention they know is
**first to three round wins**. Prigent's Cambodian ethnography describes a
**net lead of three**, in which the opponent's wins cancel earlier wins (see
[07-thai-lao-khmer-local-variants.md](07-thai-lao-khmer-local-variants.md)).
These are different rules and must not be merged.

| convention | ends when | after decisive rounds A, B, A, A |
| --- | --- | --- |
| first to 3 wins | either player has 3 wins | A has won, 3–1 |
| net lead of 3 | the difference reaches 3 | no winner yet; A leads by 2 |
| 3 consecutive wins | one player wins 3 in a row | no winner yet |
| best of 3 | either player has 2 wins | A won at round 3 |

For illustration only: with independent, equally likely decisive rounds, the
expected match length is 4.125 rounds for first-to-three, 9 for a net lead of
three, and 2.5 for best-of-three. These are properties of the stopping rules,
not of how people actually play. Ties add throws.

> The 4.125 and 9 follow from standard results: first-to-*n* in a fair series,
> and a symmetric random walk absorbed at ±3, whose expected time is 3² = 9.

A Thai–Cambodian difference in scoring does not show national uniformity on
either side, or diffusion between them. The owner's observation has no recorded
locality or age cohort yet.

## Five readings of what a hand game does

They are not exclusive, and none is established for this game:

1. **Rehearsal:** low-stakes practice in conflict, strategy, role negotiation
   and uncertainty.
2. **A present-day institution:** the duel *is* a collective decision
   procedure, not only training for one.
3. **Peer culture:** children adapt and pass on gestures, chants and rules
   among themselves; adults need not be the source.
4. **Fairness and authority:** a formally symmetric choice can legitimise
   unequal roles, and age or status can override it.
5. **Luck and fate:** players experience outcomes they do not control. That
   does not license inferring beliefs about destiny.

Keep these terms apart: **stochasticity** (a model of outcomes; RPS players
choose strategically, so the game is not intrinsically random), **luck** (a
participant's attribution), **fate** (a culturally specific idea of destiny),
**fairness** (whether the procedure is accepted as legitimate), and **power**
(who must submit to the game, and who enforces its result).

**Inference, not finding:** *one two som* may work as a small institution that
authorises impartial decisions, keeps peer relationships intact and marks local
identity. Watching how children invoke, bend, dispute and obey it would test
this better than collecting names.

## Two research lineages

### Ethnographic and developmental: episodes, peer morality, chants

- Opie & Opie (1959, 1969): children's oral culture and games; a European
  comparison, not a regional source.
- Sutton-Smith, *The Ambiguity of Play* (1997); Corsaro, "interpretive
  reproduction" (1993 and later): children as social actors who build their
  own repertoires.
- Prigent (2013), *Moussons* 22: Cambodian hands-and-mouth games, including an
  RPS-like duel and its scoring. See note 07.
- Prigent & Wendling (2025), and Prigent (2025), *Cahiers de littérature
  orale* 97: games, orality and peer morality (fairness, cheating, yielding).
- Lew-Levy & Amir (2024; 2026 response), *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*:
  children as agents of cultural adaptation.
- Petrollino (2024/2025), *Journal of African Cultural Studies*: children
  passing on linguistic and ecological knowledge through herding games.
  Outside this region, but useful for method.

### Comparative and cultural-evolutionary

- Roberts, Arth & Bush (1959), "Games in Culture", *American Anthropologist*;
  Roberts & Sutton-Smith (1962, 1966). This is the conflict-enculturation
  tradition. Its correlations do not show that games cause the abilities they
  correlate with.
- Leisterer-Peoples et al. (2021), "The Austronesian Game Taxonomy",
  *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*, with its dataset
  (AustroGames), and the analytical companion paper in *PLOS ONE* (2021).

What AustroGames is (the 907 and 168 figures were checked against the papers
on 2026-11-06; the rest is from the handoff):

- the taxonomy paper codes 907 rule-based games from Austronesian-language
  communities, drawing mainly on English- and German-language sources;
- the paper says the game descriptions are "available upon request", but its
  Data records section also points to them on Zenodo (record 4675217) and
  GitHub. Check there first;
- the *PLOS ONE* companion analyses 168 games from 25 ethnolinguistic groups;
- coders agreed well on goal structure but poorly on whether a game was local
  or introduced;
- it is not a census, so a game missing from the dataset was not necessarily
  missing from the community;
- it covers few Thai, Lao or Khmer (Tai-Kadai and Austroasiatic) communities.

It is useful here for its practice of linking every game to a source, flagging
uncertainty and handling non-independence between related cultures (Galton's
problem). It cannot reconstruct an RPS transmission path, and its associations
are observational.

## Guardrails this note adds

- Do not confuse "first to three wins" with a "three-win lead".
- Do not infer beliefs about fate from statistical unpredictability.
- Do not read a causal training effect into a cross-cultural correlation.
- Do not take a language-family tree for the family tree of a game.
