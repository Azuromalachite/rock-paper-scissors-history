# 7. Thailand, Laos and Cambodia: local names, versions and scoring

Mostly from the research handoff of 2026-11-06
([docs/handoffs/2026-11-06-thread-handoff.md](../docs/handoffs/2026-11-06-thread-handoff.md)),
re-checked against the sources on the same day where `evidence/sources.yml`
says so. Where that check disagreed with the handoff, this note follows the
source.

**Do not read this as a map of regions.** Most of the evidence is isolated
first-person comments or one editorial list. A regional name can coexist with
the national name เป่ายิ้งฉุบ in the same town, school or generation.

## The 2006 Dek-D list

Dek-D, "เป่า ยิ้ง ฉุบ ในแต่ละที่", dated 07/02/49 (7 February 2006), credits its
list to love4home.com. Its entries:

| locality | name given | what the article says it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the North | โต โป้ง | the ordinary hammer–scissors–paper game |
| Korat (Nakhon Ratchasima) | โอโป้ง | the ordinary game |
| Surat Thani | ชี โพ่ง | the ordinary game |
| Songkhla | วัน ตู ส้ม | a game with bird, water, stone **and scissors**, said to resemble Malaysia's วัน ทู ซุม |

For the Malaysian game, the same article describes five signs (bird, stone,
gun, board, water) with win rules. This is a re-locatable source for the
five-sign version that [03-southern-thailand.md](03-southern-thailand.md)
otherwise knows only from a book seen second-hand.

Dated comments push back on the list:

| date | locality | what the commenter says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 8 Feb 2006 | Ubon Ratchathani | it is called จาโป้ง there |
| 8 Feb 2006 | Korat | they had not known Korat calls it โอโป้ง |
| 28 May 2006 | not given | วัน ตุ ส้ม is played before hide-and-seek |
| 2 Apr 2006 | the North | it is called โอวาแป๊ะ (perhaps a palm-up/palm-down procedure, not the cyclic game; the comment does not say) |
| 18 Mar 2007 | Songkhla | surprised by the list; they use the ordinary name |

So, by early 2006, **the chant name วัน ตู ส้ม was attested for Songkhla**,
in an editorial list that a Songkhla reader then disputed. The article does not
name Hat Yai. Its Songkhla sign set has four signs, not the three-sign
bird–stone–water of later reports. Neither the article nor its source says
where love4home.com got its list.

the catalogue text only; the film itself has not been watched. It belongs to
UNESCO's *Traditional Children's Games in South-East Asia* box set, which is a
priority archive for Thailand, Laos and Cambodia.

## Laos: a gap, not a uniformity

No Lao description with its full gestures and scoring was found. UNESCO's Luang
Prabang material and Lao childhood ethnographies are leads, not confirmations.
Search in Lao script, in Thai/Isan, and in French colonial and anthropological
records, and separately for Tai Lue, Khmu, Hmong and other communities. A blank
on the map is missing data.

## Cambodia: Prigent's ethnography

Steven Prigent (2013), "Rythmes et euphonies dans les jeux mains-bouche. Un
zoom ethnographique sur la sociabilité entre enfants (Cambodge)", *Moussons*
22: 125–151, studies children's chants, hand-clapping and hand duels in a
village in Kompong Cham province, called Thnaot Chum in the article. The author
notes he changed the village's name.

| feature | what the article records (OpenEdition ¶) |
| --- | --- |
| name | transcribed *p̎āv ŝīṅ ŝuṅ*, glossed "envelopper, couper, marteler" ("wrap, cut, hammer"); the author suggests a Cantonese/Teochew origin (¶8) |
| signs | hammer (closed fist), leaf (flat hand, palm up), knife or scissors (index finger extended, with the middle finger) (¶8) |
| scoring | wins cancel: A then B returns the score to zero, and A, A, B gives A 1–0. Play continues until one player reaches 3. This is a **net lead of three** (¶9) |
| use | it opens circle games (choosing the leader), ends paired duels (the winner then strikes the loser), and appears in catching and pawn games (¶10–12, ¶49) |

The duel is mostly **part of other games**, opening, settling or closing them,
rather than a game played for its own sake.

Prigent (2025), "Premières tensions morales entre pairs", *Cahiers de
littérature orale* 97, takes up peer morality: fairness, cheating, yielding and
keeping relationships. Its abstract names the village **Cheung Kok**. Since the
2013 name was a pseudonym, do not assume the two are the same site, or a
longitudinal sample, without reading the fieldwork sections.

Prigent's suggested Cantonese/Teochew origin for *p̎āv ŝīṅ ŝuṅ* is relevant
to the Thai เป่ายิ้งฉุบ etymology question in
[02-thailand.md](02-thailand.md). It is another resemblance, not a sound
history.

## Scoring: first to three is not a lead of three

The owner's own experience of Thai play is **first to three round wins**.
Prigent's Cambodian rule is a **net lead of three**. These are different
stopping rules (table in
[08-play-cultural-packages-and-theory.md](08-play-cultural-packages-and-theory.md)).
Neither is shown to be national. The owner's observation still needs a
locality and age cohort, and the question to ask anyone is: "If A wins, then B,
then A twice, has anyone won?"
