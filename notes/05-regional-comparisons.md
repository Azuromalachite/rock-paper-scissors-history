# 5. Indonesia, the Pacific and India

The evidence here concerns several different things: a game's history, how
communities interpret it, and how children use it to organise play.

| region | hand game | what it helps explain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Indonesia | *suit / suten*: elephant–human–ant | a different symbolic vocabulary for the same three-way cycle |
| Hawaiʻi | *jan ken po*, with localised chants | migration and transformation of Japanese play into shared local culture |
| Aotearoa/New Zealand | Māori *tākaro ā-ringa*, including *hei tama tū tama* | hand games within indigenous social, linguistic and recreational traditions |
| India (Tamil contexts) | *saa boo three* and other selection procedures | children using hand displays to assign roles |

## Indonesia: the closest comparison to bird–stone–water

| gesture | represents | defeats |
| --- | --- | --- |
| thumb | elephant (*gajah*) | human |
| index finger | human (*manusia/orang*) | ant |
| little finger | ant (*semut*) | elephant |

The heritage organisation Traditional Games Returns documents these rules and
distinguishes the game from *batu–gunting–kertas*, also called *suit Jepang*
("Japanese suit"). The name alone does not establish when or how the Japanese
version arrived.

Research article: Dewi Oktaviani and Dewi Kinasih, "Suten Games: Exploration of
Empirical and Theoretical Probability for Junior High School Mathematics
Learning", *Ethnomathematics Journal*, doi:10.21831/ej.v4i1.59575. Qualitative
and ethnographic; documents elephant–ant–human as a fair-determination
procedure and examines its probability structure. It studies a living practice,
not its origin.

Rock, paper and scissors are not necessary to the game: elephant–human–ant and
bird–stone–water implement the same cyclic relation. No dated record resolves
whether the Indonesian version preceded or was borrowed from the Malay or
southern Thai ones.

## Hawaiʻi: a direct cultural-history example

Dennis M. Ogawa, *Jan Ken Po: The World of Hawaii's Japanese Americans*
(University of Hawaiʻi Press; paperback listed 1982). The publisher presents the
game as brought from Japan and absorbed into wider local culture, juxtaposing
the Japanese chant with its localised form ("Junk An'a Po, I Canna Show").

Relevance to วันตูส้ม: a chant can change pronunciation and acquire a local
identity while the game keeps circulating. This does not, by analogy, prove the
proposed English/Malay etymology of วันตูส้ม, and Hawaiian janken is not
evidence that ancient Polynesian societies played rock–paper–scissors.

## Aotearoa/New Zealand: different mechanics

New Zealand's Ministry of Education documents *hipitoitoi*, *hei tama tū tama*
and *whakaropiropi ai*, noting that terms and practices vary between hapū and
iwi, and placing recreational games in the context of post-harvest leisure and
traditions associated with Raukatauri and Raukatamea.

*Hei tama tū tama* uses four upper-body and arm positions; players try to catch
an opponent making the same position. It is a **matching** game, not a
three-choice dominance cycle.

Older ethnography: Elsdon Best, *Games and Pastimes of the Māori* (1925); the
indexed contents include "The game of ti ringa". Bibliographic record and
contents verified, chapter not examined.

No comparably strong evidence was found for early RPS in Fiji, Samoa, Tonga or
Micronesia; Hawaiʻi cannot stand in for all of them.

## India: selection games, weak evidence on early RPS

*Saa boo three* is a palm-orientation selection procedure, closer to Thai
โอน้อยออก or Indonesian *hompimpa* than to elephant–human–ant. Contemporary
Tamil documentation shows it used to choose a catcher or leader. Neither
establishes its age.

Source-quality warning: Wikipedia cites a 2015 *Times of India* article "Saa
Boo Three, for the child in you" for this; on inspection that article is about
a television game show and does not support an ancient origin.

Not supported by current evidence: that RPS originated in India, that
elephant–human–ant came from India, or that these games spread into Southeast
Asia with ancient Indian influence.

## Three layers to compare

1. **Rules**: cyclic dominance, matching, or palm-up/palm-down selection.
2. **Local expression**: animals, objects, gestures, chants, explanations.
3. **Social use**: who starts, assigning an unwanted role, settling a dispute,
   competing for fun.
