# Three-way hand games across Asia and the Pacific: historical, ethnographic, and cultural-evolutionary research handoff

**Date of synthesis:** 6 November 2026  
**Project:** [Azuromalachite/rock-paper-scissors-history](https://github.com/Azuromalachite/rock-paper-scissors-history)  
**Scope:** Comprehensive synthesis of the conversation, including its changes in research direction, evidence, competing interpretations, literature, and outstanding verification tasks.  
**Status:** Research notes, **not** a settled history or a systematic review.

> **Essential correction:** The Thai match convention described by the user is **first to three round wins**, not a *net lead of three*. Steven Prigent's Cambodian ethnography describes a **net lead of three**, in which the opponent's wins cancel earlier wins. These must never be silently merged. Their co-occurrence within a broader regional game family is a question for further research, not proof of transmission.

## Executive synthesis

The inquiry began as a search for three-way hand games resembling rock–paper–scissors (RPS) in East Asia, Africa, and Indigenous North and South America. It then narrowed to older games in **East Asia, Southeast Asia, and the Pacific Islands**, and increasingly centered on southern Thailand and the Malay Peninsula. The central historical finding is not that one national version has been identified, but that **multiple partly overlapping local repertoires** exist: rock–paper–scissors (hammer/scissors/paper); older Japanese frog–slug–snake and fox-based *ken* games; Indonesian elephant–human–ant; Malay and southern Thai bird–water–stone; and Malaysian/Penang/Phuket glass or cup–water–stone. Separate traditions of palm-up/palm-down selection, matching games, numerical finger guessing, and rhythm/chant games need to be compared without treating them all as three-way cyclic contests.

The central conceptual conclusion evolved as well. Hand games are not only formal sets of winning rules. They are **situated cultural practices** embedded in chants, gestures, peer hierarchies, turn allocation, dispute settlement, language contact, memory, social norms, and expectations about fairness. A game may function as a small social institution in the present, not merely as preparation for adult institutions in the future. The idea that games train people to navigate uncertainty, cooperation, conflict, and fate has a serious scholarly lineage, but the causal claims remain unsettled.

**The most promising working hypothesis** is a historically connected **Malay–southern Thai peninsular repertoire**, with particular attention to the Penang–Phuket Hokkien/Baba contact network. Evidence exists for closely matching **glass–water–stone** versions and the *one two som / วันตูโส้ม* name family; the direction, date, and actual carriers of transmission have **not** been demonstrated. The similarity can also reflect recurring exchange and local semantic reinterpretation of conserved gestures. The gesture–symbol relationship may be especially revealing: joined fingers can depict **bird**, **cup**, or **glass** while the hand duel keeps a similar cyclic structure.

For a serious history, the best unit of study is a **dated observation of a practice in a particular community**—not “one game per country.” Each observation should keep separate: the local name and chant; gesture forms; represented symbols; dominance relation; match/scoring rules; the larger game in which the duel is embedded; local social meaning; who taught whom; source date versus recollected play period; and uncertainty.

## 1. How the research question changed

| Stage | Question | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| I. Global comparison | Did peoples in Africa or Indigenous Americas also invent three-choice hand games? | Distinguished cyclic-dominance games from superficially similar guessing, coordination, and matching games. |
| II. Older Asia-Pacific record | What do premodern and nineteenth-century accounts actually describe? | Prioritized Chinese and Japanese *ken*, Ryukyuan/Amami variants, Malay games, and early Polynesian ethnography. |
| III. Malay Peninsula | Is *one two som* (glass–water–stone) a Penang–Phuket cultural variant? | Identified Malaysian documentation and Penang-specific reminiscences, alongside Phuket/Krabi reports. |
| IV. Theoretical reframing | Can play be separated from its cultural package? | Shifted from symbol genealogy to rules, institutions, fairness, child peer cultures, and transmission. |
| V. Mainland diversity | Are central, northern, northeastern Thai, Lao, and Khmer versions actually uniform? | Located named regional variants, community ethnographies, UNESCO documentation, and a Cambodian scoring distinction; Laos remains underdocumented. |
| VI. Scholarship and corrections | What is the literature lineage, and what precisely differs in match scoring? | Connected Prigent's ethnography to AustroGames and cultural-evolution research; corrected Thai match scoring to first-to-three. |

### Three analytically distinct comparisons

1. **Exact cyclic dominance:** three simultaneous options, each defeating one and losing to another (e.g., rock–scissors–paper; elephant–human–ant).
2. **Other simultaneous hand procedures:** matching, finger-number guessing, palm-orientation selection, etc. These may share transmission channels or social uses but **not** the same game-theoretic mechanism.
3. **A full play episode:** the procedure may choose a catcher or leader, settle a dispute, open or end another game, redistribute teams, or interact with age hierarchy. This social unit can be more historically informative than the hand duel alone.

Do not equate geographic presence with independent origin; an old-looking local version might be borrowed, while an externally borrowed game may be deeply embedded in local practice.

## 2. East Asia: older *ken* traditions and limits of the chronology

### China and Japan

- Historical Chinese hand and finger games form part of the comparative background, but the popular “Han dynasty origin of modern RPS” claim is **not demonstrated** by the frequently cited *Wuzazu* (五雜組) passage. Xie Zhaozhe's Ming-era text mentions a **手勢令** and admits uncertainty about how it worked. The historical episode involving Shi Hongzhao belongs to the **Later Han of the Five Dynasties (tenth century)**, not the Han imperial dynasty of 206 BCE–220 CE. No modern RPS rules are supplied in the passage.
- Japan's older **三すくみ拳 (*sansukumi-ken*)** repertoire uses three-way symbolic domination but not always rock/scissors/paper. **狐拳 (*kitsune-ken*)** juxtaposes fox, village head, and hunter; **虫拳 (*mushi-ken*)** juxtaposes frog, slug, and snake.
- Historical Japanese illustrated materials discussed in the thread include a **1774** *kitsune-ken* illustration and an **1809** *mushi-ken* illustration. A separate scholarly claim cites a **1756** printed reference for *mushi-ken*; the specific document and scholarly argument should be rechecked before assigning a definitive “first.”
- An estimate that stone/scissors/paper *ishi-ken* emerged **1804–1843** derives from **Kako Satoshi (2008)** as relayed through a **Yokohama Central Library answer** in Japan's National Diet Library Collaborative Reference Database. It is an **estimate**, not a securely dated firsthand occurrence.
- **Stewart Culin (1895), *Korean Games, with Notes on the Corresponding Games of China and Japan***, supplies a firmer nineteenth-century historical document for Japanese three-symbol play; the repository's first adversarial review reports checking pp. 44–45. Confirm edition and text when citing exact gestures.
- Older *mushi-ken* has sometimes been connected to a Chinese snake–frog–centipede symbolic cycle, with Japanese slug substitution proposed as an interpretive/linguistic transformation. This is an explanation worth tracing in early Chinese and Japanese texts, not an established single-step transmission event.

### Korea, Ryukyu, and Amami

- Korean **가위바위보 (*gawi-bawi-bo*)** uses scissors, rock, and wrapping cloth (*bo*, associated with *bojagi*). **묵찌빠 (*muk-jji-ppa*)** demonstrates how one familiar gesture repertoire can support a different contest mechanism, with attacker/defender roles and victory when matched gestures occur on the attacker's turn.
- **Okinawa/Amami:** *būsa / būsa-shī* and Okinoerabu **さみ (*sami*)** were identified as related local frog–slug–snake or comparable cyclic games. Reported local meaning of particular gestures can differ (including substitution of a *habu* snake), and oral documentation includes chants and intergenerational memories. Their exact subregional distribution needs item-by-item archival verification.
- **Historical takeaway:** the symbols, the hand shape, the chant, the matching/dominance mechanism, and the match rules can have partially distinct histories. Modern Japanese RPS should not be projected backward onto every historically recorded *ken*.

**Starting scholarship:** Sepp Linhart (1995), “Some Thoughts on the Ken Game in Japan: From the Viewpoint of Comparative Civilization Studies,” [DOI 10.15021/00003009](https://doi.org/10.15021/00003009); Culin (1895); Japanese illustrated books and National Diet Library reference answers.

## 3. Southeast Asia: distinctive symbolic cycles

| Tradition | Typical signs and winning cycle | Most important caution |
|---|---|---|
| Japanese-style RPS | rock > scissors > paper > rock | Do not assume all other cycles derive from this exact symbol set. |
| Indonesian *suit / suten / pingsut* | **elephant (thumb) > human (index) > ant (little finger) > elephant** | Living customary form is documented; early date and independent invention are unresolved. |
| Malay/southern Thai bird variant | **bird > water > stone > bird** (bird drinks, stone sinks, stone strikes bird) | Source-specific regional forms and social uses matter; do not generalize to Malaysia or southern Thailand as a whole. |
| Malaysian/Penang/Phuket cup or glass variant | **glass/cup > water > stone > glass** (contains/scoops water; stone sinks; stone breaks glass) | A particularly close comparison, but transmission route/direction and Penang ethnic exclusivity remain unproven. |
| Bird–gun–water and multi-symbol accounts | Reported in Trang, Nakhon Si Thammarat, Phatthalung; related materials mention board, gun, glass, or brick | Reconstruct full matrix, gesture set, and whether accounts combine multiple games before coding a five-choice or four-choice system. |

### Indonesia

In *suit/suten/pingsut*, the thumb represents **gajah** (elephant), the index finger **orang/manusia** (human), and the little finger **semut** (ant). The ant defeats the elephant in folk explanation despite their unequal sizes, illustrating the cyclic principle. A modern ethnomathematics study documents the practice and its probability structure (Dewi Oktaviani & Dewi Kinasih, [DOI 10.21831/ej.v4i1.59575](https://doi.org/10.21831/ej.v4i1.59575)). It does **not** determine its ancient origin or relation to peninsular *osom*.

### Malaysia, Penang, Phuket, and Krabi: the strongest contact hypothesis

**Three distinct evidence strands:**

1. **Malaysia, 2002 documentary attestation.** Michael Wong, “Malaysia in Our Classroom: Activities” (Fulbright-Hays teaching materials; ERIC **ED471818**, printed p. 461, “One Two Som”), gives **glass–water–stone** with hand signs, rule descriptions, and the name. [ERIC full PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED471818.pdf). A Malaysian location is established, but **Penang specifically is not assigned by this source**.
2. **Penang-linked retrospective testimony, January 2005.** A [Chinese-language/Hokkien forum discussion](https://www.chineselanguage.org/forums/viewtopic.php?start=10&t=1611) recalls **som tri** and **one two som**, and discusses whether the name and game were characteristic of Penang or of Hokkien/English educational backgrounds. The accounts describe cup/sea(or water)/stone with compatible gestures and rules. **Status:** dated online recollection, not an ethnographic sample or proof of ethnic origin.
3. **Phuket/Krabi oral and online reports.** The project repository's [`03-southern-thailand.md`](https://github.com/Azuromalachite/rock-paper-scissors-history/blob/main/03-southern-thailand.md) documents **วันตูโส้ม**, stone as fist, water as open palm, and glass as fingers gathered into a cup. Phuket and Krabi localities come largely from a Facebook post/comments held as **private screenshots**; they are **not publicly independently auditable** in the repository. A purported entry in *พจนานุกรมภาษาถิ่นภูเก็ต พุทธศักราช 2560* remains **not located in the book**. Catalogue records confirm the dictionary exists (publication date catalogued as 2559/2016 despite 2560/2017 in the title).

**Historical context, distinct from transmission evidence:** Khoo Salma Nasution (2009), *Hokkien Chinese on the Phuket Mining Frontier: The Penang Connection and the Emergence of the Phuket Baba Community*, describes nineteenth-century Penang–Phuket commercial, migration, kinship, and Hokkien/Baba connections. This makes the corridor plausible, **but the historical network is not direct evidence that it carried this game**.

**Related formal evidence:** the ASEAN Committee on Culture and Information's *Compilation of Traditional ASEAN Children's Songs, Dances, Games and Story-Telling* (2009), Malaysia section, pp. 86–87, records **One Two Som / One Two Juz** using **bird, stone, water**, a chant, simultaneous display, and replay on a tie. The repo's adversarial review located an Internet Archive capture of the 2009 volume (2025-05-20); it is stronger support than unspecified contemporary artist reminiscences. The same volume describes **Lai Lai Li Tham Plom**, a palm-up/palm-down selection practice, distinct from cyclic three-sign play.

**Other potentially relevant nomenclature:** Malay **ohsom / osom / wan tu som** appears in a 7 September 2018 Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka enquiry; **oh bey som** is described in Singapore's *BiblioAsia* as palm-up/palm-down grouping, not necessarily bird–stone–water; similar syllables alone cannot establish etymology or common rules.

**Specific hypothesis:** the **gesture configuration** may have persisted while the symbol represented by joined fingers shifted between **bird**, **cup**, and **glass**. Test by collecting detailed gesture videos, childhood learning histories, and dated written illustrations. Avoid inferring whether the bird or glass interpretation was ancestral.

### Early Malay ethnography as a useful negative search result

D. F. A. Hervey, “Malay Games” (1903), *Journal of the Anthropological Institute* 33:284–304, is available via the [Wellcome Collection](https://preview.wellcomecollection.org/works/weqtqs57). According to the repository's independent review, reading pp. 284–304 found **no *osom*-type hand game**; reported selection methods included concealed-seed guessing, drawing lots, and tossing shells/coins. **The absence from this single sample does not imply absence from Malaya in 1903.**

R. J. Wilkinson, *Life and Customs: Malay Amusements* (1925), is a priority unread primary/secondary historical source: do not claim it contains *osom* before inspecting the relevant pages.

## 4. Pacific Islands: older simultaneous gestures need not be cyclic RPS

**Tonga — *li‘agi*.** John Martin's *An Account of the Natives of the Tonga Islands*, based on William Mariner (1817 first edition, volume II, pp. 339–341), offers a striking early record: two or four participants make rapid, simultaneous **open-palm, closed-fist, or index-finger** gestures. The score depends on **matching or failing to match** and on remembering/signifying sequences, **not** a three-way cycle of who beats whom. The account associates it with chiefs and *matāpule*. [Digitized primary text](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/Ancillary/BeagleLibrary/1817_Mariner_A710.02.html). Do not call it “early Pacific rock–paper–scissors.”

**Samoa — *lupe*.** Nineteenth-century reports associated with the 1838–1842 U.S. Exploring Expedition describe simultaneous finger-number matching and quick decisions. It belongs to a comparative genre of hand games, but exact cyclic-dominance mechanics have **not** been shown.

**Māori — *tī/ringa*, *hei tama tū tama* and related games.** Ethnographic and New Zealand educational materials document a variety of gesture/matching games; the latter can use **four positions and matching**, not the three-option dominance matrix. Consult Elsdon Best, *Games and Pastimes of the Māori* (1925), and contemporary Māori educational sources, taking care with iwi/hapū differences.

**Hawaiʻi.** Japanese *jan ken po* appears in Japanese American and wider local culture, including localized chants; this is an instructive documented adaptation, **not evidence for pre-contact Polynesian RPS**. Dennis M. Ogawa, *Jan Ken Po: The World of Hawaii's Japanese Americans*, first published **1973**, second edition **1978** (a 1982 publisher listing is not the first edition).

**Methodological value of these comparisons:** simultaneous gesture, fixed number of signs, short chants, uncertain outcomes, and social allocation can recur without cyclic dominance. All are relevant to the *history of practices*, but coding them as one single game would distort the historical map.

### Initial worldwide comparison, now secondary

Africa (San *n!à*, Ghanaian *Ampe*, Yoruba-area *tẹn-tẹn*) and Indigenous American hand, bone, and dice games were considered early in the thread. Their reported mechanics are usually rhythm/matching, leg selection, concealment/guessing, or chance—not yet established independent three-sign cyclic dominance. Culin's *Games of the North American Indians* (1907) is a particularly broad traditional-hand-game source. **No definitive negative historical claim** is warranted: the search was exploratory, archives are uneven, and the project has since been intentionally narrowed to Asia and the Pacific.

Its evidence often consists of isolated first-person recollections or editorial lists, not surveys. A regional term can coexist with the nationwide Thai name in the same locality, generation, or school.

### Laos: a conspicuous evidentiary gap

This thread did **not** identify a defensible locally specific Lao cyclic-hand-game description with its complete gestures and scoring rules. UNESCO's Luang Prabang documentation and Lao childhood ethnographies are leads—not confirmations. Search in **Lao script**, Thai/Isan, and French colonial or anthropological records, and separately include Tai Lue, Khmu, Hmong, and other community-specific sources. An unfilled area on the map is **missing data**, not historical uniformity.

### Cambodia: Steven Prigent's detailed ethnography

**Prigent (2013),** “Rythmes et euphonies dans les jeux mains-bouche. Un zoom ethnographique sur la sociabilité entre enfants (Cambodge),” *Moussons* 22:125–151, [DOI 10.4000/moussons.2385](https://doi.org/10.4000/moussons.2385), studies children's chants, handclapping, gesture duels, rhythm, competition, cooperation, and sociality in a rural Cambodian community. The RPS-like game is transcribed as **p’āv ŝīṅ ŝuṅ** (romanization as rendered in the source). Reported signs include **hammer/closed fist**, **paper/open flat hand**, and **knife or scissors/extended finger(s)**.

The duel frequently **opens, mediates, or concludes other games**, rather than necessarily being played on its own. Children can use it to select a leader, but existing age hierarchy may allow older children to lead without a duel. The source also describes chanting, rhythmic acceleration, and sometimes playful hitting, pinching, or tickling at the conclusion. These features make it an unusually strong example of the game as a component of peer relations rather than an abstract decision rule.

**Verification of scoring from original French:** A reproduced full-text passage on [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/272648279_Rythmes_et_euphonies_dans_les_jeux_mains-bouche_Un_zoom_ethnographique_sur_la_sociabilite_entre_enfants_Cambodge) says if A wins the first round and B the second, **the score returns to zero**; if A wins twice and B once, A leads **1–0**. The count continues until someone reaches **three net wins**. Thus the earlier reading of the Cambodian rule as a **three-win advantage** is supported. If citing verbatim, verify the printed article's transcription and page (the passage appears around p. 142 in the reproduced text).

A later article, **Prigent (2025)**, “Premières tensions morales entre pairs: Jouer avec les mots et les mains, à l'âge enfantin, dans un village cambodgien,” *Cahiers de littérature orale* 97, [DOI 10.4000/144w3](https://doi.org/10.4000/144w3), examines peer morality: fairness, strategic action, cheating, yielding, and maintaining relationships. **Caution:** the 2025 abstract names a village **Cheung Kok**; earlier discussion identified the 2013 field site as **Thnaot Chum**. Do not presume these are the same site or longitudinal sample without checking the fieldwork sections.

## 6. Match-scoring correction: distinguish four systems

**Authoritative user clarification in this thread:** In the user's Thai experience, the rule is **first player to accumulate three round wins**. Previous messages occasionally described this as net-three, which was **incorrect for the user's observation**.

| Match convention | Exact condition | Sequence of decisive winners **A, B, A, A** | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First to 3 wins** | `wins(A) >= 3` or `wins(B) >= 3` | **A wins**, 3–1 | **User-observed in Thailand**; localities/ages not yet recorded. |
| **Net lead of 3** | `abs(wins(A) - wins(B)) >= 3` | No winner yet; A's net lead = 2 | **Prigent's Cambodian description**, with wins canceling losses. |
| **Three consecutive wins** | One player wins three decisive rounds in a row | No winner yet | A third distinct convention; do not collapse with either above. |
| **Best of 3** | First player to 2 wins | A has already won after round 3 | Distinct from first to 3 wins. |

Ties are commonly replayed/ignored, but should be independently documented for each context.

**Mathematical comparison (illustrative only):** Under independent equal-probability *decisive* rounds, the expected number of rounds is **4.125** for first-to-three wins; **9** for a net-three-lead symmetric random walk; **2.5** for best-of-three. These calculations describe formal stopping rules, **not** actual human strategies, social meanings, or empirical play durations. Ties would increase the count of physical throws.

**Research consequence:** scorekeeping conventions can vary even when names and symbols coincide; **round**, **match**, and **whole play episode** must be separate levels of coding. Do not use a Thai–Cambodian match-rule contrast to assert national uniformity or diffusion.

## 7. Games as cultural packages and social institutions

### Main conceptual claim

The user's intervention—“We cannot distinguish play from the cultural set/package”—reoriented the work. A game is inseparable *in practice* from the children and other players who perform it, the relationship it mediates, the languages spoken, norms about fairness, and established procedures for deciding who begins, loses, or takes an unwanted role. Nevertheless, for comparison **researchers may analytically disaggregate** the package into mechanisms that have potentially different transmission histories. Analytical disaggregation does **not** imply that these features circulate in isolation.

This yields at least five possible interpretations, not exclusive:

1. **Enculturation / rehearsal:** Games may provide low-stakes practice in conflict, strategy, role negotiation, cooperation, and responses to uncertainty.
2. **Present-day institution:** The hand duel is itself a real collective decision procedure, not merely training for one.
3. **Peer-culture production:** Children adapt gestures, chants, and rules and transmit them horizontally; adults need not be the sole source.
4. **Fairness and authority:** A formally symmetric choice can legitimize unequal roles, while age/status may override the procedure.
5. **Fate, luck, and contingency:** Games allow people to experience outcomes that are not fully controlled; it is unsafe to equate probabilistic unpredictability with cultural beliefs about destiny.

### Distinguishing uncertainty concepts

- **Stochasticity:** a probabilistic model of outcomes. RPS is not intrinsically random; players choose strategically. Uniform-independent sign choice produces symmetry only under assumptions.
- **Luck:** a participant's attribution to chance or limited control.
- **Fate:** a stronger culturally situated conception of destiny/providence; cannot be inferred from hand-game presence alone.
- **Fairness:** social legitimacy of the allocation process. Even a strategically nonrandom game may be accepted as fair if both parties agree on the procedure.
- **Power:** when age, status, group norms, or leadership modify who must submit to the game and how the result is enforced.

### Relevant frameworks

- **Roberts, Arth & Bush (1959), “Games in Culture.”** Foundational comparison of games of physical skill, strategy, and chance with social organization.
- **Roberts & Sutton-Smith (1962), “Child Training and Game Involvement,” and (1966), “Cross-cultural Correlates of Games of Chance.”** The conflict-enculturation tradition: possible links among games, child-rearing, uncertainty, and social conflict. Correlates do not prove training causally produces later abilities.
- **Brian Sutton-Smith (1997), *The Ambiguity of Play*.** Multiple “rhetorics” including progress, fate, power, and communal identity; refuses reduction of all play to future utility.
- **Gregory Bateson (1955/1972), “A Theory of Play and Fantasy.”** Metacommunication: signals that frame actions as play; relevant to negotiated interpretations and pretend consequences.
- **Johan Huizinga (1938), *Homo Ludens*; Roger Caillois (1958), *Les jeux et les hommes*.** Classic play typologies and relations between play and social life; foundational, not contemporary empirical tests of RPS transmission.
- **Corsaro (1993 and later), “interpretive reproduction.”** Children creatively appropriate and transform the cultures in which they participate.
- **Hannes Rakoczy (2007), “Play, games, and the development of collective intentionality.”** How shared norms and conventions emerge/are understood during rule-governed play.
- **Thomas Malaby (2007), “Beyond Play: A New Approach to Games.”** Games as domains of contingency embedded in social life.
- **Roberte Hamayon (2012), *Jouer: Une étude anthropologique*.** Anthropological treatment of playing as a form of acting and relating, not merely a detached simulation.

**Inference, not demonstrated fact:** *one two som* might be a mini-institution that authorizes impartial decisions, preserves peer relationships, and reproduces local identities. Observing how children *invoke, modify, dispute, and obey* it would test that interpretation better than cataloguing names alone.

## 8. The two major research lineages and recent work

### 8.1 Ethnographic/developmental lineage: episodes, peer morality, chants

- **Opie & Opie (1959, 1969):** children's oral culture, schoolyard lore, and games, a broad European documentary comparison rather than the direct regional historical source.
- **Sutton-Smith; Corsaro; Delalande:** children as meaningful social actors with locally created peer repertoires.
- **Prigent (2013):** close Cambodian description of *mains-bouche* (hands-and-mouth) games with physical rhythm, speech, RPS-like duels, and social contexts. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4000/moussons.2385).
- **Prigent & Thierry Wendling (2025):** thematic issue introduction, “Jeux, Oralités et Enfances,” *Cahiers de littérature orale* 97. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4000/144vz).
- **Prigent (2025):** moral tensions between child peers, cheating, fairness and relationship maintenance. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4000/144w3).
- **Lew-Levy & Amir (2024 online):** “Children as agents of cultural adaptation,” *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*. Children actively create and maintain peer cultures. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X24001377).
- **Lew-Levy & Amir (2026):** “Toward an integrated study of peer cultures,” a published response synthesizing commentaries and further research directions. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X2510424X).
- **Sara Petrollino (2024 online / 2025 issue):** “Herding Games and Socialisation into Pastoral Linguacultural Practices,” *Journal of African Cultural Studies* 37:86–110. A field case demonstrating children transmitting ecological and linguistic knowledge during Hamar herding-play, outside the Asia-Pacific geographic case study but directly relevant to methods. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/13696815.2024.2324879).
- **Anatoli & Cekaite (2025):** “Everyday poetics and language play in young children's interactions in a bilingual institutional context,” *Discourse Studies*. Useful analog for chanting and linguistic reanalysis; inspect original full text before making detailed claims. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614456251372997).

### 8.2 Comparative anthropology / cultural phylogenetics lineage

- **Roberts, Arth & Bush (1959)** → **Roberts & Sutton-Smith (1962, 1966)** → **Eifermann (1970)** → later cross-cultural comparative work on games, cooperation, hierarchy, and subsistence.
- **Austronesian language-family phylogenetics:** Gray, Drummond & Greenhill (2009) and ABVD; **Pulotu** database of Austronesian supernatural beliefs/practices (Watts et al. 2015); **D-PLACE** (Kirby et al. 2016); **eHRAF** ethnographic archives. These provide ways of recording shared ancestry/cultural covariates, but *language genealogy is not automatically the genealogy of games*.
- **Leisterer-Peoples, Hardecker, Watts, Greenhill, Ross & Haun (2021), “The Austronesian Game Taxonomy.”** *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* 8:113. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-021-00785-y); [GitHub](https://github.com/ccp-eva/AustroGames); [Zenodo 4675217](https://zenodo.org/record/4675217). This is the **user-uploaded PDF** (`s41599-021-00785-y.pdf`), a dataset/methods paper rather than a causal test of enculturation.
- **Leisterer-Peoples, Ross, Greenhill, Hardecker & Haun (2021), “Games and enculturation: A cross-cultural analysis of cooperative goal structures in Austronesian games.”** *PLOS ONE* 16:e0259746. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0259746). Uses the relevant comparative data to examine associations between game goal structures and socio-ecological variables.

### 8.3 What the uploaded Austronesian Game Taxonomy actually does

**Dataset architecture:** historical/ethnographic descriptions from eHRAF, Pulotu bibliographies, *American Anthropologist*, *Journal of the Polynesian Society*, and other sources; original searching limited primarily to **English and German**. Published data cover **907** identified rule-based games in Austronesian-language communities. “Descriptions”, “Games”, “Cultures”, and “Sources” are linked with IDs, and named fields track geographic uncertainty, game/goal uncertainty, whether origin is thought local/introduced, and available ethnolinguistic identifiers.

**Paper's Table 5 samples (do not mix these up):**

| Stage | Games | Note |
|---|---:|---|
| Linked candidate descriptions | 952 | Prior to game-definitional filtering. |
| Qualified as games | **907** | Headline size of dataset. |
| Assignable ABVD cultural identifier | 764 | Covers 79 groups. |
| Assignable goal structure | **521** | Covers 68 groups. |
| In the paper's Figure 2 visualization after game, ABVD, goal, and phylogeny-related filters | **452** | Covers **55 ethnolinguistic groups**. |
| Example subset matched to tightly time-aligned Pulotu data (±0) and several other filters | **53** | Covers 10 groups. |
| Same with ±50-year focus window | **172** | Covers 27 groups. |

**Six goal-structure types:** solitary; individual competitive; competitive-vs-solitary; competitive-vs-cooperative-group; cooperative-group-vs-cooperative-group; and wholly cooperative group. Figure 1 visualizes social interdependence. Coding reliability was high after refinement (**Cohen's κ = 0.94** for goal structure), but origin/locality coding was more difficult (**κ = 0.487** for local/nonlocal/unknown; binary introduced/nonintroduced κ = 0.808). **This matters for any attempt to infer game origins from the database.**

**Access limitation:** CSV data and R code are openly available, but the paper says full verbatim game descriptions are **available on request because of copyright restrictions**. The dataset is not a census of all games. A recorded lack of a hand game in one culture is not evidence that it was never played there. The dataset's Austronesian coverage does **not** systematically describe most Thai, Lao, or Khmer (Tai-Kadai/Austroasiatic) communities.

**The analytical companion paper:** **168 games, 25 ethnolinguistic groups**; reported associations included intergroup conflict with more cooperative-game goal structures and intragroup conflict with more competitive structures. Models are observational and associations may weaken in multivariable specifications; selection and coverage biases remain. This study cannot prove that playing a given game trains adult behavior, nor can it be used to reconstruct a specific RPS transmission path.

**Why AustroGames is nevertheless important:** explicit source-provenance, game linkage, ethnolinguistic coding, uncertainty flags, temporal focus, and ability to compare distribution while recognizing non-independence (Galton's problem). These principles can inform the local hand-game atlas even if its categories are too broad for chants, gestures, and match-scoring rules.

## 9. A research atlas centered on practices, not national labels

### Proposed data model: three nested observation levels

**Round:** two or more players' simultaneous signs, hand shape, symbol, ordering, tie treatment, immediate winner/loser.  
**Match:** number of rounds, stopping rule (first-to-three, net-three, three-consecutive, etc.), score reset, rematches and multi-player elimination.  
**Social episode:** why the match is invoked; larger enclosing game; whose authority it determines; whether everyone accepts the result; bypasses, rule negotiation, and disputes.

**In parallel**, record locally meaningful features independently:

| Field | Why needed |
|---|---|
| `locality` and spatial uncertainty | Avoid treating province/nation as the natural unit. |
| `speaker_birth_year`, `childhood_year_range`, `field_date`, `publication_date` | Recollection date ≠ play date ≠ document date. |
| `self_described_community` and languages used | Understand multilingual transmission without assigning an ethnicity by place alone. |
| `game_name_original_script` and `chant_audio_or_transcript` | Detect sound change, code-switching, reanalysis, or borrowing. |
| `gesture_video`, `symbol_mapping` | Bird vs cup/glass may preserve a hand shape. |
| `win_loss_matrix` | Distinguish true cyclic games from matching and ambiguous multi-symbol reports. |
| `stopping_rule`, `tie_rule`, `score_keeping_words` | Captures the Thai/Cambodian distinction and other match-level features. |
| `social_function` | Choosing catcher, leader, teams, starts, consequences, or entertainment. |
| `who_taught_whom` and school/neighborhood context | Tests vertical, horizontal, and oblique transmission. |
| `norms_and_disputes` | Legitimacy, cheating, fairness, age hierarchy, strategic yielding. |
| `source_id`, `locator`, `access_mode`, `confidence`, `negative_search_scope` | Auditability; do not turn hypotheses into observations. |

**Possible schema choice:** one `observation_id` per reported practice × place × cohort × period × source, even when two observations share a common game-family ID. Avoid giving a “canonical” local version unless independent evidence supports population-level prevalence.

### Phylogenetic and network questions

- **Vertical inheritance vs diffusion:** adjacent communities can share innovations through multilingual schools, trade, migration, kinship, or children visiting each other. A language-family tree alone cannot model every such transfer.
- **Transmission as a network:** Penang–Phuket Hokkien/Baba links, Malaysian school communities, Hat Yai/Songkhla, mainland Thai schools, Cambodian border localities, and migrant communities can be mapped as historically changing contact networks. But network edges must represent documented contacts, **not claimed game transmission**.
- **Trait-specific genealogies:** names/chants, gestures, symbol semantics, winning matrices, and match-scoring can show discordant histories. Test co-inheritance rather than assume one tree for the whole cultural package.
- **Independent innovation:** shifting bird to cup or glass is cognitively plausible, but plausibility alone does not establish convergence. Compare detailed hand shapes, local explanations, histories of learning, and the breadth of the symbol/gesture correspondences.
- **Institutional persistence:** if symbol sets change yet social role-allocation norms remain, the object of continuity may be the accepted decision procedure rather than the iconography. Treat this as a hypothesis.

### Survey design (practical, falsifiable)

1. Collect in-person or recorded demonstrations from several **age cohorts** per locality; ask people to *perform* rather than merely name the game.
2. Record **two-person, three-person, and team contexts** separately, asking whether the practice is used to select players, assign duties, or settle a disagreement.
3. Ask the exact question “If A wins once, B wins once, then A wins twice, who has won, if anyone?” to discriminate stopping rules; ask how ties affect count.
4. Separate “What did you play as a child?” from “What do children play here now?” and “What do you think is the local traditional name?”
5. Record birthplace, childhood residence, schools, languages, and known learning source; do not infer ethnicity from the respondent's location.
6. Preserve original-language recordings/transcripts and explicit consent/permissions. Avoid identifying private online commenters or releasing screenshot material without permission.
7. Pre-register what would count as evidence *against* a Penang–Phuket transmission hypothesis or against stability of a gesture mapping. A result that shows widely mismatched gestures despite similar names would weaken certain interpretations.

### Suggested search geography

**Priority corridor:** Penang ↔ Kedah/Perlis ↔ Satun/Songkhla/Hat Yai ↔ Trang ↔ Krabi/Phuket ↔ Nakhon Si Thammarat and Surat Thani.  
**Comparators:** northern Thai/Tai Yai/Tai Lue, Ubon/Korat/Sisaket (including Kui/Kuay), Laos (Luang Prabang and border communities), Cambodia (Prigent's field sites and Thai-border communities), Singapore, Indonesian *suit*, Taiwan/Ryukyu, Japan.  
**Pacific comparative controls:** Tonga *li‘agi*, Samoa *lupe*, Māori matching games, Hawaiian Japanese-derived *jan ken po*.

These are **research priorities**, not proposed or demonstrated directional diffusion arrows.

## 11. Questions still open—and what would answer them

| Open question | Evidence needed | What *would not* be sufficient |
|---|---|---|
| Did glass–water–stone spread Penang → Phuket, Phuket → Penang, or through repeated contact? | Dated pre-2002 illustrations/texts, independently documented recollections with play periods, teacher/peer migration histories | Matching modern names plus known migration links alone. |
| Is the *one two som* chant an English-derived phrase or an older local form? | Early texts, sound histories, original scripts, language-specific syllable and rhythm analysis | Modern resemblance to “one, two” or to Japanese *jan-ken-pon*. |
| Did bird become glass/cup or vice versa? | Earlier localized sign descriptions; demonstration recordings; lexical/metaphorical associations | Cognitive plausibility of one interpretation. |
| What was the earliest Thai/Siamese RPS-style game? | Dated pre-1989 Thai printed material (and catalogs), schools/manuals/newspapers/dictionaries, physical copies | A WWII origin story from an unsourced 2008 article. |
| How variable are Thai names and stopping rules? | Systematically sampled cohorts across regions and ethnic/linguistic communities | One comment per province, or a modern national dictionary. |
| Does Lao play have locally distinctive versions? | Lao-script and French/Thai ethnographic records, UNESCO games materials, interviews | Assuming *bao ying chub* or national uniformity in the absence of data. |
| Is the Cambodian net-three method localized or widespread? | Additional Khmer field sites; compare nearby Thai/Lao practices, terms for score cancelation | The presence of “three” in a scoring rule in another country. |
| Do games actively *cause* learning of cooperation/fairness? | Longitudinal/intervention or natural experiments with cultural context controlled | Cross-cultural correlation of games and social attributes alone. |
| Do children transmit rules more independently than symbols/chants? | Peer diffusion studies, age-stratified observations, bilingual school networks, repeated game demonstrations | Treating “the game” as a single inherited trait. |

A candidate overarching question for the project is:

> **How are culturally recognized procedures for making and legitimizing decisions under uncertainty transmitted, reinterpreted, and embedded in children's peer worlds across connected East and Southeast Asian societies?**

The RPS family provides a tractable case because its **compact cyclic rules** can be compared while its names, symbols, gestures, performance, and match conventions vary.

## 13. Evidentiary safeguards and explicit corrections from the conversation

1. **Do not call an attestation an origin.** A 2002 Malaysian textbook does not show first invention in 2002, nor an origin in Penang.
2. **Do not infer ethnic invention from contact history.** The Penang–Phuket Hokkien/Baba connection supports plausibility of exchange, not proof that a group invented the game.
3. **Do not let a national label erase local variation.** “Thai” or “Cambodian” is too coarse a unit; an observed scoring rule is not automatically standard across the nation.
4. **Do not fuse different hand-game families.** Cyclic domination, matching, numeric finger guessing, and palm-up/palm-down selection can be historically related but are structurally distinct.
5. **Do not confuse three wins with three-win lead.** This distinction was explicitly corrected by the user and cross-checked against Prigent's French original.
6. **Do not infer fate beliefs from statistical unpredictability.** Fairness, luck, fate, social power, and stochasticity must be treated separately.
7. **Do not equate an archival absence with a cultural absence.** Early published game surveys are selective and unevenly sampled.
8. **Do not imply causal training from observed correlation.** AustroGames' socio-ecological association is a comparative hypothesis test, not an intervention showing children become fairer or more cooperative.
9. **Keep the game within its complete cultural episode.** Even if a computer-readable game matrix is useful, the larger social practice remains the anthropological object.
10. **Preserve source access levels.** A DOI landing page, catalog entry, forum post, and directly read passage warrant different claims.

### Final framing

The project is no longer simply **“Who invented rock–paper–scissors, and how did it spread?”** Its more fruitful form is **“How have communities across East and Southeast Asia transmitted and transformed culturally legitimate procedures for decision-making, competition, and peer coordination?”** The accessible details—three signs, chants, stop rules, social use—provide a concrete empirical entry point into larger questions of historical diffusion, children's agency, normativity, chance, and cultural evolution.
